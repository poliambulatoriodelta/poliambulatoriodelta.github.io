Heart, kidneys and metabolism: the new approach to cardiometabolic health

TD
Poliambulatorio Delta Team
· 20 August 2026 · 8 min read

Why preventing cardiovascular disease today means looking beyond the heart.

When we talk about cardiovascular health, we immediately think of the heart, blood pressure and cholesterol. But today we know that cardiovascular risk often begins much earlier and involves a wider system, in which heart, kidneys and metabolism are closely linked.

This awareness is what gave rise to the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome (CKM). In June 2026, for the first time, the American Heart Association, the American College of Cardiology, the American Diabetes Association and the American Society of Nephrology published a guideline specifically dedicated to the prevention, diagnosis and management of CKM syndrome. The aim is to move beyond a fragmented approach and consider a person's health as a whole.

What does CKM syndrome mean?

The term CKM brings together three areas that seem different but are deeply interconnected:

  • C – Cardiovascular: heart and blood vessels
  • K – Kidney: kidneys
  • M – Metabolic: metabolism, obesity and diabetes

A change in one of these systems can increase the risk of problems in the others. For example, excess weight and insulin resistance can lead to diabetes and high blood pressure; these conditions can progressively damage the kidneys and, at the same time, increase the risk of heart attack, stroke and heart failure. Similarly, chronic kidney disease is not only about kidney function: it is also a major cardiovascular risk factor. The heart, therefore, cannot be considered in isolation.

CKM syndrome stems precisely from the need to recognise these connections and act before organ damage becomes evident.

Risk can begin long before disease

One of the most interesting aspects of the new approach is that there is no need to wait for a heart attack, kidney failure or established diabetes to appear. The new CKM classification considers different levels of risk and makes it possible to follow how the condition evolves over time. A patient may be at an early stage characterised, for example, by overweight or obesity and progressively develop metabolic changes, high blood pressure, kidney damage or cardiovascular disease.

The good news is that the path is not necessarily one-way: acting early on weight, diet, physical activity, blood pressure, blood glucose and other risk factors can help slow or, in some cases, positively change the progression.

Which warning signs should not be underestimated?

There is no single symptom that identifies CKM syndrome. Often, in the early stages, there are no symptoms at all. This is why it is important to pay attention to conditions and risk factors that appear unrelated: overweight and obesity, increased waist circumference, high blood pressure, abnormal blood glucose or HbA1c, high cholesterol and triglycerides, diabetes, reduced kidney function or the presence of albumin in the urine.

In some people, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), sleep-disordered breathing and early signs of altered heart function can also be important. The new guideline recommends a systematic assessment of the main metabolic risk factors and of kidney function, with targeted further tests when indicated.

One patient, several specialists: why a multidisciplinary approach is needed

CKM syndrome highlights a limitation of the traditional approach: treating separately organs and risk factors that are, in reality, closely linked. This is why the new guideline places particular emphasis on interdisciplinary care.

It is not just about having more tests

A CKM pathway does not simply mean undergoing numerous checks. Above all it means putting the information together. A high blood pressure reading, raised blood glucose, a large waist circumference or a slight reduction in kidney function can take on a different meaning when they are considered as a whole.

The new guideline also introduces a more personalised approach to cardiovascular risk assessment, through the PREVENT equations, used to estimate cardiovascular risk at 10 and 30 years and to inform decisions about prevention. The aim is therefore to move from medicine that intervenes when disease appears to medicine increasingly geared towards personalised prevention.

Treatments are changing too

The new CKM model is also changing the way we think about treatments. Today some treatments can offer benefits that go beyond the single parameter for which they were initially developed. In people with type 2 diabetes and certain risk profiles, for example, some GLP-1-based therapies and SGLT2 inhibitors can contribute to cardiovascular and kidney protection, in addition to metabolic control. The choice of treatment must of course be personalised according to each person's characteristics and conditions, as assessed by the specialist.

This is one of the most important changes in cardiometabolic medicine: not just treating the abnormal value, but protecting at the same time the organs most exposed to risk.

Prevention begins before you feel ill

Perhaps the most important message of CKM syndrome is exactly this. You do not need to wait until you have a heart problem to take care of your heart. And you should not wait for kidney function to decline significantly before starting to protect your kidneys.

Prevention can begin with the assessment of a few fundamental elements: weight, blood pressure, blood glucose, lipids and kidney function, combined with your medical and family history and any further tests recommended by your doctor. It is a simple but important change of perspective: do not wait for heart, kidneys and metabolism to become ill before looking after their health.

An integrated pathway for cardiometabolic health

Cardiometabolic medicine was created with exactly this aim: to connect what is too often assessed separately. This is why Poliambulatorio Delta is the ideal place to build a multidisciplinary pathway, in which cardiology, endocrinology and nutrition work together for an overall assessment of the patient.

The result is not simply a sum of specialist consultations, but a coordinated pathway of prevention and care, built around the person and their risk profile. Because taking care of the heart today also means taking care of the kidneys and metabolism. And, above all, it means acting before risk becomes disease.

Prevention rather than cure: the check-up and the tests that matter

TD
Poliambulatorio Delta Team
· 9 October 2026 · 6 min read

High blood pressure, high cholesterol and diabetes can stay silent for years: by the time symptoms appear, the damage has often already begun.

That is what prevention is for: finding a value that is out of range while it still takes very little to correct it.

Why prevention pays off more than cure

A check-up done while you are healthy costs less time, less money and less worry than treatment started late. An abnormal value discovered early can often be corrected with diet, exercise or mild treatment.

Prevention has two sides, and both are needed:

  • Lifestyle: a balanced diet, regular physical activity, no smoking, alcohol in moderation, enough sleep.
  • Regular check-ups: simple tests that give a snapshot of your health and flag in good time what you cannot feel.

The starting point, for an adult who has not had tests for a while, is a basic check-up.

The basic check-up: a complete snapshot in a single appointment

A basic check-up generally brings together four elements: a general medical examination, an electrocardiogram, spirometry and blood and urine tests. Together they assess heart, lungs, metabolism, liver and kidneys.

None of these tests, on its own, makes a diagnosis. It is the doctor, during the examination, who reads the results together and tells you whether everything is fine or whether further investigation is needed.

Routine check-ups, age by age

The checks that are useful change with age: at 30, a few baseline tests are enough. This is a general outline, which the doctor adapts to each person's history.

From 20 to 39

  • Have your blood pressure measured at least once every three years.
  • Have basic blood tests (full blood count, blood glucose, cholesterol) at least once: they will be your baseline over the years.
  • If there are cases of heart attack, diabetes or cancer at a young age in your family, talk to your doctor: check-ups should start earlier.

From 40 to 49

  • Have your blood pressure measured every year.
  • Check blood glucose, cholesterol and triglycerides: with these values the doctor estimates your cardiovascular risk.
  • Have an electrocardiogram and, if indicated, a first preventive cardiology consultation.
  • If you smoke or have smoked for a long time, add spirometry.

From 50 to 69

  • Continue blood pressure, blood and heart checks at the intervals recommended by your doctor.
  • If you are a man, discuss with your doctor whether a prostate check is appropriate.
  • If you are a woman, after the menopause discuss bone health with your doctor.

After 70

  • Keep up regular checks of blood pressure, kidney function and full blood count.
  • Periodically review the medicines you take with your doctor.
  • Do not neglect hearing, balance and the vaccinations recommended for your age.

How often to repeat check-ups

There is no interval that suits everyone: it depends on age, previous results and personal risk factors.

  • Blood pressure: at least every three years under the age of 40, at least once a year from 40 onwards, according to the 2024 European cardiology guidelines.
  • Blood tests: the interval is set by your doctor. Normal values allow longer intervals; borderline values call for closer checks.

Who should start check-ups earlier

  • People with close relatives who had a heart attack, stroke, diabetes or cancer at a young age.
  • People who smoke or have smoked for a long time.
  • People who are overweight or do little exercise.
  • People who have already had borderline blood pressure, blood glucose or cholesterol.
  • Women who had diabetes or high blood pressure during pregnancy.

Signs you should not put off

Prevention is for people who feel well. Chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, unintended weight loss, blood in the stool or urine, tiredness that does not go away: in these cases see a doctor straight away, without waiting for your next check-up. In an emergency, call 112.

How to prepare for the check-up

A few simple precautions are enough to get reliable results. Ask for confirmation of the instructions when you book.

  • Come having fasted for at least 8 hours: you may drink water.
  • Bring your previous tests and a list of the medicines you take. Do not stop any treatment unless your doctor tells you to.
  • In the hours beforehand, avoid smoking, coffee and intense effort: they can alter blood pressure, the electrocardiogram and spirometry.
  • Wear comfortable clothes that are easy to open at the chest for the electrocardiogram.

After the check-up: what happens

The doctor explains the results and tells you the next step. There are three possible outcomes:

  • Everything is normal: you set the date for your next check-up.
  • Some borderline values: you receive advice on diet and exercise and repeat the test after a short interval.
  • A finding to investigate: the doctor refers you to a specialist.
